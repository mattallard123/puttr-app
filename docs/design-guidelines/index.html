Design review and three directions

Your read is right. The four tabs were built at different times and each one settled on its own look, so the app mixes five different surface treatments and has no single rule for where 3D belongs. Below is what I found on each tab, why it feels inconsistent, and three ways to fix it, each shown on the first page of all four tabs.

Decided, 24 November: Proposal A, Broadcast

The main button is Puttr's brand green, the same as Order Puttr on puttr.co. Improve leads with 3D stroke sessions. Profile has no main button: editing is an icon on the avatar and Switch is a secondary button. The mockups below show these. The rules are written up in docs/design-guidelines.md and apply to every new feature.

Reviewed from this morning's Mac build with sample data, phone size, top of each tab and three screens of scroll, plus the style sheets.

What each tab does today

Home

  • The only tab with the logo bar. Its session card uses a gradient fill, a green outline and a sentence-case title.
  • Main button is dark green with white text. Improve and Profile each use a different green.
  • One scroll shows five kinds of picture: 3D renders (Jump back in), a flat drawn map (Tracks), a stock photo (Tournaments), a patterned icon tile (You might like) and plain bordered cards (Live, League, Challenges).
  • Four chip styles inside two cards: grey, green outline, amber fill, orange streak.
  • The Switch pill sits on top of the greeting row. The scroll ends in an empty screen.

Play

  • The most consistent tab: one tile, three tags, one title style.
  • The art inside the tiles is not consistent. Some are an empty dark scene, some a close-up, and some were captured mid-game with score digits and gauges in the shot (Major League, Speed Demon).
  • Tiles have a raised bottom edge. Almost nothing else in the app does.
  • Tags float over the art and collide with it.
  • No title or logo, only a text tab strip. Search is a faint 34 pt circle, under the 44 pt minimum touch size.
  • A paragraph at the top explains what the tab is.

Improve

  • The strongest first screen: a live 3D mat with a big italic title.
  • The title runs under the Chat pill, and a second pill is cut off at the right edge.
  • Its button is bright lime with dark text and a glow. No other tab has a glowing button.
  • The same session is on Home as a completely different card.
  • Lower down: a flat score card with a 2D ring, then a live 3D window, then rounded 3D practice tiles, then empty grey video boxes with no thumbnails.

Profile

  • Has its own look entirely: green halo behind the avatar, scan lines on the stat strip, a sheen on the membership card, neon glowing calendar cells and a glowing chart.
  • Three icon styles: glossy 3D medals (Achievements), tilted pseudo-3D rings (Make % by break) and flat tinted squares (Competition).
  • Challenges here is a different card from Challenges on Home.
  • No header. Two equal-weight buttons at the top, one lime, with a stray "Admin" label under them.

Why it reads half flat, half 3D

There are five materials in use, and no rule for which one a new component gets:

  • Flat bordered cards: lists, League, Challenges, the score card.
  • 3D render windows with a raised edge: game tiles, Jump back in, Practice.
  • Glow and neon: the Profile hero, calendar, charts, the Improve button.
  • Glossy objects: medals and tilted rings.
  • Photos and drawings: the tournament photo, the Tracks map.

The games worth copying each pick one material for the interface and give 3D one job. Sports titles keep the menus flat and angular and save 3D for the pitch. Tap-heavy mobile games make every control a chunky physical object. Console home screens put one live scene behind a quiet overlay. Apple's own apps use solid dark surfaces, one accent colour, capsule buttons and a 44 pt minimum for anything you tap. The three proposals below are those three answers applied to Puttr.

Proposal A

Broadcast

The idea. The interface is a sports broadcast graphic: flat, solid, sharp-cornered, with heavy italic numerals. 3D only ever appears inside a framed window, like a replay on a scoreboard.

The rule for 2D and 3D. No interface element has depth: no raised edges, glows, gloss or gradients. A 3D picture always sits in a rectangular window with its text on a solid bar beneath it, never over it.

Borrowed from. Football and racing titles' menus, and Apple Sports: scoreboard tables, slanted buttons, labels in wide-spaced caps.

What changes most. Profile loses its glow and becomes a Player card and a stat table. Medals become flat crests. Tags move off the art.

Proposal B

Clubhouse

The idea. Everything you can press is a physical object. Buttons, cards, chips and tiles all share one raised bottom edge and sink when tapped. Rounder, warmer, more playful.

The rule for 2D and 3D. One depth rule for the whole interface: a solid edge underneath, lit from above, no blur. 3D game art fills its tile and takes the same edge, so art and controls look like the same kind of thing.

Borrowed from. The big tap-first mobile games and learning apps: chunky buttons, sticker tags, reward gold for personal bests.

What changes most. Home, Improve and the lists get heavier and more colourful. The existing medals and the Play tile edge already fit. It moves furthest from the sports direction you set on 16 November.

Proposal C

Stage

The idea. Every tab opens on one live 3D scene, the stage, with a quiet flat sheet of content sliding up over it. Improve already works this way. This makes it the pattern for all four tabs.

The rule for 2D and 3D. One world per screen. 3D is the stage and nothing else; everything on the sheet is flat, solid and borderless, in the style of Apple's own apps, with capsule buttons and a floating tab bar. Game lists use small thumbnails so they do not compete with the stage.

Borrowed from. Console home screens and game lobbies, with Apple's grouped lists underneath.

What changes most. Play swaps its wall of large tiles for a featured game and a compact list. Home and Profile each need a scene built. It costs the most, and older devices would need a still picture in place of a live scene.

What I recommended: A, with C's stage kept where it already works

A is the direction you already chose for games, carried into the tabs. Its rule is the simplest to hold a build to: the interface is flat, and 3D is always in a window. It is also the least work, because most of it is collapsing the style sheets onto one set of values. A and C share the same flat material, so Improve can keep its stage under A, and Home could gain one later without another redesign. I would not pick B unless you want the app to lean arcade first and coaching second.

Rules I would set whichever you pick

These fix the drift on their own and would go into memory with the chosen direction.

  • One main button, brand green with white text, at most one per screen. Everything else is the grey secondary.
  • One header on all four tabs: the tab's name on the left (the logo on Home), up to two actions on the right, nothing overlapping it.
  • One component per kind of content. Today's session, a challenge, a game and a stat each have a single design used on every tab.
  • One chip with two states, on and off. Colour only for meaning: amber for time running out, red for live, gold for a personal best.
  • Type: Montserrat only, six sizes. Black Italic caps for titles and numbers, 700 wide-spaced caps for labels, as already decided.
  • Spacing in steps of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, with a 16 pt side margin. Three corner radii at most.
  • Colour: brand green for fills, bright green for small text and icons, five greys, three meaning colours. Solid only, since see-through colours render too bright in this project.
  • Card art is shot clean: no in-game score or gauges in the picture, and a clear area kept free for the title.
  • Nothing you tap is under 44 pt.
  • No glow, sheen, scan lines or photos. Never the same information twice on one screen.

The mockups are drawn in HTML. The mat scenes are stand-ins for the real 3D engine, and the game pictures are the app's current card art.
